How to pop your ears on a plane

A three-minute lesson from Dr. Michael H. McDonald, an ear, nose and throat surgeon from the University of Wisconsin, on a gentler and more controlled way to clear your ears.

Prefer YouTube? Watch it there.

The method, step by step

Dr. McDonald recommends the Toynbee maneuver. It uses your tongue and a swallow instead of a hard blow, so you can be precise about how much pressure you apply.

  1. Pinch your nostrils shut

    Use your thumb and finger, the same way you would for any ear-popping technique.

  2. Exhale gently against your pinched nose

    Gently is the point. The upper part of your nose should flare just a little.

  3. Trap the pressure with your tongue, then pump it up

    Squeeze your tongue against the roof of your mouth to raise the pressure. If you are doing it right, you will feel the pressure in your fingertips as your tongue moves.

  4. Swallow

    As you swallow, push your tongue against your soft palate. Swallowing triggers the muscles that open the Eustachian tube at the same moment the pressure is up.

  5. Repeat until the pressure is gone

    It may take more than one pump. You will usually hear a pop or a crackle when it works.

If only one ear clears

Plug the cleared ear firmly with your index finger and repeat. This directs the pressure to the ear that is still blocked.

Why your ears plug when you fly

Going up

Usually no trouble

Pressure outside the eardrum drops, and the air behind it bulges the eardrum outward. You may feel plugged or hear a little less, but the middle ear is built to vent this without effort.

Coming down

The hard part

Pressure outside the eardrum rises and squeezes it inward. If it does not equalize, it can cause pain, bleeding, or fluid in the middle ear that can take several weeks to clear.

It is also why babies so often cry during descent. It hurts, and they cannot fix it on their own.

Why not just pinch and blow?

Pinching your nose and blowing hard is the usual advice. It is called the Valsalva maneuver, and it does work. The catch is where the pressure comes from: the large muscles of your abdomen and chest.

It is possible to push too hard

Dr. McDonald knows of at least two patients who overshot with a hard Valsalva and ruptured an eardrum. The tongue-and-swallow method above is much easier to control.

About Dr. McDonald

Michael H. McDonald, MD, is an otolaryngologist (ENT surgeon) from the University of Wisconsin. In this video he explains the technique he recommends for clearing your ears safely during a flight.
